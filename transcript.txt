
00:00:00 | A frontier for every height
Source: Both abstracts; September introduction

A zero-free frontier
You already know what the Riemann hypothesis says. So our question is more specific: what would it mean to exclude every zero to the right of one fixed vertical line? We will explain the claims of these two manuscripts, and then actually derive where eleven twelfths comes from. This is an explanation of their argument, not an independent proof audit.

The picture is finite. The theorem is not.
Write a complex number as sigma plus i t. Sigma is horizontal; t is vertical. This surface shows actual numerical values of the zeta function, with height equal to log of one plus its magnitude. Valleys mark small values. The highlighted point is the first known nontrivial zero. A finite plot illustrates the function, but cannot establish a statement at every height.

One boundary, independent of height
Classical zero-free regions narrow toward the line sigma equals one as the height increases. These papers claim fixed boundaries: eleven twelfths and, more strongly, seven eighths. The second excludes a wider half-plane. Reflection symmetry would then confine the nontrivial zeta zeros between one eighth and seven eighths. The critical line remains a much stronger target.


00:01:18 | Why the zeros know about primes
Source: October §1; classical Euler product

Multiplication becomes analysis
For sigma greater than one, zeta is both a sum over all positive integers and a product over primes. Expanding each geometric series in the product lists every integer exactly once, by unique prime factorization. This is the entry point for primes. Inside the critical strip we use analytic continuation; the displayed infinite product is not being asserted to converge there.

Characters separate residue classes
A Dirichlet character is a periodic multiplicative weight. For example, this character modulo four assigns zero to even integers, plus one to integers congruent to one, and minus one to integers congruent to three. Inserting these weights gives an L function. Comparing such weighted prime sums helps separate primes in different arithmetic progressions.

A family-wide claim
The manuscripts claim their boundary for every Dirichlet character, not just the trivial character that gives zeta. The same numerical boundary works independently of the conductor and the height. That does not mean every constant in the proof is uniform: constants may depend on the fixed target character. It is the exponent, and ultimately the boundary, that must remain common.


00:02:36 | Turn zeros into a cancellation problem
Source: October §§2.1, 3

The arithmetic inverse
The Möbius function is zero when a square of a prime divides the integer. Otherwise it is minus one to the number of distinct prime factors. Thus six has value plus one, thirty has value minus one, and twelve has value zero. For sigma greater than one, replacing each Euler factor by its inverse produces the series for one over zeta.

Cancellation measures how much is left
Let M of x be the cumulative sum of these signs. This curve is computed from actual Möbius values, not a simulated random walk. The trivial bound is order x. A bound of x to the power alpha, with alpha less than one, would quantify cancellation. We cannot infer that bound from a short plotted sample.

A zero would force a pole
Partial summation writes the reciprocal of zeta as s times an integral of M of x against x to the power minus s minus one. If M grows no faster than x to alpha, the integral converges locally uniformly when sigma exceeds alpha. It therefore defines a holomorphic function there. But a zero of zeta would force its reciprocal to have a pole. Those two facts cannot coexist.


00:03:53 | Why move to the Eisenstein lattice?
Source: October §§1.4, 2.1–2.2

An arithmetic setting with sixfold symmetry
The argument works in the number field Q of square root minus three. Its integers are a plus b omega, where omega is a cube root of one. In the complex plane they form this triangular lattice. The norm is a squared minus a b plus b squared. The geometry makes cubic and sextic residue characters natural tools for the problem.

The same inverse, over ideals
The paper sums over nonzero integral ideals, with an ideal version of Möbius and a finite-order Hecke character, nu. You can think of nu as the multiplicative phase weight in this larger arithmetic setting. A smooth window W selects norms near a scale D. The resulting sum A one has roughly D terms. Primes above two and three and the conductor are excluded as specified in the paper.

Introduce a tunable phase
Now insert a sextic residue character depending on a new variable u. Its nonzero values are sixth roots of one, shown around this circle. Changing u produces a family of sums A u. The original target is the row u equals one. The strategy is to control the combined squared size of the entire family, then recover that particular row.


00:05:12 | An average bound is not a pointwise bound
Source: October Proposition 3.1; §2.2

The manuscript's central estimate
Here is the difficult estimate the October paper claims. Sum the squared magnitudes of A u over norms up to H. The answer is at most a constant times D to one plus epsilon, times H, with H equal to D to one plus a small positive theta. Since there are order H rows, the average squared magnitude is about D, allowing a tiny exponent loss.

One large row can hide in an average
The bars are a schematic energy budget, not computed character sums. Average square-root cancellation does not say that every individual sum has size square root D. A few large outliers could fit inside the total budget. This distinction matters: extracting the target row using only a typical-row argument would be invalid.

Force the target to appear many times
The next move is to find many rows that almost reproduce A one. If A one were huge, all those rows would consume energy. The total mean-square estimate then limits its size. The sixth-power rows provide exactly this repetition. That is the bridge from an average theorem to a bound on one prescribed L function.


00:06:26 | Where eleven twelfths comes from
Source: October §2.2 and equation prime-extract in §3

Sixth powers erase the phase
Take u equal to p to the sixth power, where p is a prime element in the Eisenstein integers. Away from p, a sixth-order character raised to the sixth power is one. When p divides the summation index, it is zero. So A of p to the sixth differs from A one only through those omitted multiples. For prime norm about Y, their total contribution is bounded by order D over Y.

Count the repeated rows
A prime with norm at most Y gives a sixth-power row with norm at most Y to the sixth. Set Y equal to H to the one sixth. The prime ideal theorem supplies order Y over log Y such rows. Divide the full energy budget by this number. Squaring the omission error gives the second term displayed here. Logarithms are absorbed into D to epsilon.

Read the exponent one operation at a time
Ignoring only the arbitrarily small exponent losses, H is D to one plus theta. The first term is D times H to five sixths. Its exponent is eleven sixths plus five theta over six. Now take the square root... eleven twelfths plus five theta over twelve. The error contributes exponent five sixths minus theta over six, which is smaller.

Theta is small, but strictly positive
For any requested positive epsilon, choose theta sufficiently small, and then choose the loss in the mean-square estimate sufficiently small. This yields D to eleven twelfths plus epsilon. We have not set theta equal to zero; later recursion needs it positive. The constant may worsen as epsilon shrinks. The zero-free boundary is the open half-plane strictly beyond eleven twelfths.


00:08:13 | How cancellation excludes an actual zero
Source: October §3, proof following Proposition 3.1

Mellin transform: resolve powers
The paper uses smooth sums, so the natural transform is Mellin rather than the sharp-cutoff integral we saw earlier. Integrate A one of D against D to minus s, with measure d D over D. The cancellation bound gives convergence at large D when sigma exceeds eleven twelfths. Compact support of the window handles small D. Local uniform convergence makes the transform holomorphic.

Identify the transform where the series converges
In the original region sigma greater than one, termwise integration identifies this transform with W hat of s divided by the Hecke L function, with finitely many Euler factors deleted. These factors are nonzero in the half-plane under discussion. The identity then extends analytically. But we still need to prevent the numerator from accidentally cancelling a zero.

Choose a window that detects the suspected zero
Suppose rho were a zero to the right of eleven twelfths. Choose a nonzero, nonnegative smooth function phi supported between one and two, and set W of y equal to y to minus rho times phi of y. Then W hat at rho is the positive integral of phi over y. The estimate permits this complex window. Constants may depend on it; the exponent does not.

The contradiction is algebraic
Multiplying the continued identity by L gives L times the holomorphic transform equals W hat. At the supposed zero, the left side is zero while the right side is strictly positive. This is the contradiction! Notice what carried the reasoning: a bound on all scales and analytic continuation, not checking zeros numerically and not moving a contour across an unaccounted pole.


00:10:02 | What supplies the hard estimate?
Source: October §§2.3–2.5; schematic proof map

Poisson summation changes the viewpoint
We have derived the boundary assuming the mean-square theorem. Now we look inside its proof. Expanding the square and applying Poisson summation in the row variable replaces arithmetic positions by Fourier frequencies. Finite Fourier transforms of sextic characters produce Gauss sums. A Gauss–Jacobi identity combines these with the Möbius coefficients, producing cubic Gauss-sum coefficients.

Arithmetic coefficients become theta coefficients
Those cubic Gauss sums occur as Fourier coefficients of a cubic theta function. This supplies transformation laws that were invisible in the original sum. To use them, the paper completes squarefree indices n to indices n times b cubed. The extra cube factors are essential. This diagram is a structural map; it suppresses local factors, coprimality conditions, and auxiliary characters.

Why quadratic characters are a gain
After the theta transformation, the relevant sextic phases combine into a third power, which is quadratic. The quadratic large sieve bounds a mean square using a row-plus-column scale, M plus L, up to small powers. This avoids the additional term that can appear in a general higher-order character estimate. The gain is quantitative, not simply a new way of writing the same sum.

Completion creates a debt
The completed sum includes cube factors that were not in the target. Möbius inversion removes them. Small cube divisors can be bounded directly. Large cube divisors leave a remainder, and that remainder still has to be controlled. This is where the recursion enters. The transformation and these remainder estimates are deep inputs claimed by the manuscript; our animations do not independently prove them.


00:11:51 | A recursion that actually becomes smaller
Source: October §2.5; full auxiliary-family treatment in §§4–7

Track two scales, not just one
Call the row scale script H and the column scale X. In the initial dual problem, they are about D to one minus theta and D. A remaining cube divisor of norm B shortens the column scale to X over B cubed. Simply shortening columns is not enough, because the old row range is still present.

Two Fourier transfers shrink both scales
The paper uses two Poisson transformations, with a deliberate enlargement of an intermediate nonnegative sum. The new row scale is script H over B cubed and the new column scale is X over B cubed. Their ratio is unchanged. The normalization of the energy compensates for the factor lost during transfer, so the new target estimate has exactly the same form.

A positive theta pays for termination
The cutoff is chosen so that the remaining branches shrink the row scale by a factor bounded by D to minus two theta. For fixed positive theta, only a bounded number of stages is needed before direct counting or an empty remainder finishes the argument. This is the simplified recursion from the outline. The full proof also carries common factors, auxiliary twists, and changing smooth weights.


00:13:06 | What changes in the seven-eighths paper?
Source: September proof overview; Proposition 2.1; Parts I–II

A different, more elaborate route
The September manuscript's stronger seven-eighths argument is not obtained by changing the sixth power in our calculation. It compares two representations of a completed cubic-theta sum. Reflection bounds the sum directly. Poisson summation separates a principal signal containing one over the target L function from other character rows. The paper must control both the sum and the error between the sum and that signal.

The right edge need not contain a rightmost zero
Let beta star be the supremum of the real parts of zeros across the relevant Hecke family, with one half included in the definition. Suppose it exceeds a proposed boundary sigma zero. The displayed pair of bounds gives the signal a positive power saving below beta star. The minimum of the two margins must stay positive independently of the target character.

Continue every reciprocal past the supposed frontier
Taking a Mellin transform of this better-controlled signal continues each reciprocal L function into a common half-plane to the left of beta star. A holomorphic factor bounded away from zero prevents cancellation. By the definition of a supremum, some zero lies inside that extended region, even if no zero attains beta star. Its reciprocal would have a pole there. Contradiction.

The refinement must pay for a wider region
To reach seven eighths, the paper changes the normalized sum: it compensates selected prime factors, uses unequal averaging scales, and combines moment estimates for inverse and ordinary character polynomials. The affine exponent changes from s minus two thirds to s minus eleven sixteenths. These estimates close the argument at seven eighths. This is a map of the extra machinery, not a derivation of that exponent from our eleven-twelfths calculation.


00:14:56 | Bring the conclusion back to primes
Source: Both introductions; October §3 transfer and Corollary 1.2

Return from Hecke to Dirichlet
Why did solving the larger number-field problem answer our original question? Lift a Dirichlet character by composing it with the ideal norm. Its Hecke L function factors into the Dirichlet L function for chi and the one for chi times the quadratic character modulo three, apart from harmless finite Euler factors. Nonvanishing of the product excludes zeros of either factor. Poles at one are treated separately.

A zero's real part controls its size
In the explicit formula for weighted prime counting, a zero rho contributes a term proportional to x to rho divided by rho. Its magnitude contains x to beta, where beta is the real part. The graph shows illustrative power envelopes, not actual prime-counting errors. Bounding all the betas away from one creates a fixed power saving, with further logarithms and summation estimates handled analytically.

The gain has a precise scope
The October paper states this uniform absolute error for counting primes in reduced arithmetic progressions: x to eleven twelfths times log x. For large moduli, that error can exceed the main term, so the statement alone is not a useful relative asymptotic in every progression. Both papers also exclude Landau–Siegel zeros sufficiently close to one. They do not rule out every real zero everywhere.

Follow the chain of implications
The accessible chain is now concrete. A mean-square estimate controls a family. Sixth-power rows repeat the target. Their count produces eleven twelfths. A Mellin transform converts cancellation into holomorphy. A supposed zero then forces an impossible pole. Poisson summation, cubic theta transformations, and a scale-reducing recursion are what the paper uses to earn that initial estimate.

A stronger frontier, with a substantial gap remaining
The seven-eighths paper uses a more elaborate comparison and continuation argument to move the fixed boundary farther left. If valid, either fixed boundary is a historic advance. Neither statement forces the critical line. The central lesson is that the power of the result comes from one guarantee valid at every height and for every character.
